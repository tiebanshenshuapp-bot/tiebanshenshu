A. The system and its names

Tieban Shenshu (鐵板神數)

Evidence level: scholarly discussion

A Qing-dynasty Chinese divination system attributed by tradition to Shao Yong (邵雍, 1011–1077) of the Northern Song. Known for kaoke — narrowing a birth time to a specific ke — and for mapping derived numbers to numbered verses.

Variant writing: 鐵版神數 (版 and 板 are interchangeable here).

Common English renderings: "Tieban Shenshu", "Tie Ban Shen Shu", "Iron Plate Divine Numbers". The last is a literal translation, not an established academic term.

Note: naming is not standardised across schools or regions. A different name does not imply a different lineage, and an identical name does not imply identical content.

Tie Buzi / Tie Daoren (鐵卜子 / 鐵道人)

Evidence level: traditional account

A figure said to have revised the verses during the Qianlong–Jiaqing period (1736–1820), from whom the "Tie ban" (Iron Plate edition) name is said to derive.

Problem: his name, dates, birthplace, and even existence have no reliable primary source. He leaves no trace in contemporaneous records — which is itself suspicious for someone said to have revised twelve thousand verses.

Chunzi shu / Dading shu (蠢子數 / 大定數)

Evidence level: scholarly discussion

Two names mentioned in Ming-dynasty criticism, generally considered early forms of, or cognate with, Tieban Shenshu.

Yang Shen (楊慎, 1488–1559), Sheng'an ji (《升庵集》):

"Nowadays wandering diviners recklessly fabricate the Dading shu and Chunzi shu, falsely attributing them to Kangjie — is this not a grave slander of a former worthy?"

This is the earliest explicit criticism currently traceable.

B. Core operations

Kaoke (考刻)

Evidence level: traditional account

Subdividing the birth hour into ke (刻), then using known life facts — chiefly family information — as cross-checks to determine the correct ke.

Basic framework (details vary by school):

1 shichen (時辰) = 2 hours
      ↓ divide into 8
1 ke (刻) = 15 minutes
      ↓ subdivide into 15
1 fen (分) = 1 minute

Important: the framework above is the general traditional scheme found in many texts. But which family details are used, how they map, and how candidates are ranked differ between schools. There is no single authoritative standard.

Dingfen (定分)

Evidence level: traditional account

Subdividing within an established ke to reach a fen.

Together with kaoke, this forms 考刻定分 — the most distinctive operation in the system.

Contested point: the specific fifteen-part correspondence tables are transmitted privately. Published accounts disagree. This project publishes no such table.

Qishu (起數) — number derivation

Evidence level: traditional account

The process of converting the four pillars into a number.

Commonly described as:

Four pillars → assign numbers → derive hexagram → extract number → verse ID

Note: the specific assignments, hexagram rules, and extraction formulas are privately transmitted or proprietary. Sources differ substantially. This project implements none of them.

Taixuan shu (太玄數)

Evidence level: traditional account

One number-assignment scheme possibly used in derivation, related to the numerology of Yang Xiong's Taixuan jing (《太玄經》).

What is public: only the concept that certain stems/branches map to certain numbers. What is not: the specific values — accounts differ.

Our educational tool therefore does not implement taixuan assignment.

C. Output form

Tiaowen (條文) — verses

Evidence level: scholarly discussion

Numbered verses — the final output form of the system.

Traditional scale: commonly said to be about twelve thousand verses, divided by earthly branch into twelve volumes (子集 through 亥集).

Numbering: the range 1001–13000 is often cited in public discussion, but manuscripts differ.

Important facts:

  • There is no critical edition
  • Manuscript copies differ in wording
  • No scholarly consensus edition exists

Treat any claim of "the one correct version" with scepticism.

Mingshu (命書) — reading report

Evidence level: traditional account

The full written reading produced by the system, usually comprising verses across several categories.

This project does not generate reading reports and provides no personal analysis.

D. Related but distinct systems

Huangji Jingshi (皇極經世)

Evidence level: classical text

Shao Yong's major work, computing historical and cosmic cycles in yuan, hui, yun, shi (元會運世). It belongs to Yijing studies and historical philosophy.

Relationship to Tieban Shenshu:

  • Tradition says Tieban Shenshu "borrows" its numerological concepts
  • But the two are not the same system
  • Equating them is an oversimplification

Huangji Jingshi itself is public domain and free to read and cite.

Sizhu (四柱) — Four Pillars / BaZi

Evidence level: classical text

Year, month, day, and hour pillars, each a heavenly stem plus an earthly branch — eight characters in total, hence bazi (八字).

Critical boundary settings (sources differ):

Pillar Boundary rule
Year Begins at Lichun (立春, start of spring), not 1 January or lunar new year
Month Begins at solar terms, not lunar month boundaries
Day Changes at zi hour (23:00 or 00:00 — schools differ)
Hour True solar time vs standard time — schools differ

⚠️ These four settings alone can produce different charts for the same person. If two programs disagree, check these first.

Liuqin (六親) — six relations

Evidence level: classical text

Traditionally father, mother, siblings, spouse, children (or the four categories: parents, siblings, spouse, children).

In kaoke, relatives' zodiac signs and counts serve as verification anchors.

E. Tools and formats

Sixty-year cycle (六十甲子)

Evidence level: classical text

Ten heavenly stems combine with twelve earthly branches; yin–yang pairing yields exactly 60 combinations, cycling indefinitely.
