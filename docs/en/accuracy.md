# Is Tieban Shenshu Accurate?

| Question | Honest answer |

## 2. Why accuracy is so hard to verify

This is not a mystical problem, it is a **methodological** one. Any predictive system
must satisfy three conditions to be tested.

### Condition 1: Stated in advance, not explained afterwards

```
❌ Not a test:
   "Did you change jobs this year?"  (person says yes) → "See, accurate!"
   ↑ This is fishing, not prediction.

✅ A real test:
   On Jan 1, write it down and seal it:
   "This person will change jobs in March 2026."
   Seal it → open it in December 2026 and check.
```

**The key question**: if a system only looks accurate **after** you know the outcome,
it had no predictive power **before** it.

### Condition 2: A record of failures must exist

Any genuinely effective method produces **failures**.

If in a system:
- Only successes circulate
- Failures are never mentioned
- Or failures get attributed to "your time was wrong" or "you lacked sincerity"

then the system **cannot be tested**, and therefore cannot be confirmed.

### Condition 3: A control group

To show that Tieban Shenshu beats chance, you need something like:

```
Group A: verses derived from the real birth data
Group B: randomly assigned verses
        ↓
Ask third parties (blind to the grouping) which set fits the person better
        ↓
If there is no significant difference → the "accuracy" comes from psychology
```

**To our knowledge, no such published double-blind study exists.**

## 3. So why do so many people feel it was "really accurate"?

This is not necessarily deception — there are well-documented **psychological explanations**.

### Reason 1: The Barnum effect

People accept **vague, generally-true statements** as accurate personal descriptions.

> "You have talent but are sometimes misunderstood."
> "You appear strong, but inside you are sensitive."
> "You went through a significant setback in your youth."

These apply to **almost everyone**. In controlled experiments, when every participant is
given the **identical** "personalised" profile, over 80% still rate it as "quite accurate."

### Reason 2: Confirmation bias

The mind **remembers what fits and forgets what doesn't**.

A reading of 20 lines where 19 miss and 1 hits — most people remember the one.

### Reason 3: Post-hoc fitting through *kaoke*

This is specific to Tieban Shenshu and is **the point to watch most carefully**.

The *kaoke* (考刻) procedure is: cast the number from birth time → then ask
"what zodiac is your father?" "how many siblings do you have?" → **use those answers
to adjust the time calibration**.

**The problem**: if you adjust the calibration using facts you already know, then the
verses you end up with matching those facts is **inevitable** — it was not predicted.

```
This is like:
  First asking "how old are you?"
  Then filling in "your birth year"
  Then announcing "I calculated your age — impressively accurate!"
```

> ⚠️ This is **not** to say *kaoke* is a fraud. Traditionally, its purpose genuinely was to
> pin down the true *ke*. But understand: **accuracy after calibration and accuracy before
> calibration mean very different things.**

### Reason 4: Selective memory and social transmission

People tend to:
- Share "it came true" stories socially
- Not share failures (it's embarrassing)
- Listeners only ever hear the successes

Over time a circle forms a collective impression that "this is accurate" — but the
**sample has been filtered**.

### Reason 5: Vague language allows self-interpretation

Well-written verses are often **interpretable either way**:

> "Hardship in early years; gradually improving in middle age."

- If things did improve → "So accurate!"
- If they didn't → "It says *gradually* — hasn't arrived yet."

**Any outcome can be read as a match.**

## 4. Does it still have value?

**Yes — but not predictive value.**

| Type of value | Exists? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Predicting the future | ❌ Unverifiable | No reproducible evidence |
| Self-understanding | ⚠️ Limited | Useful as a prompt for reflection |
| Cultural study | ✅ Yes | Reflects Qing-era ideas about fate, family, ethics |
| Literary value | ✅ Yes | The verses are rhymed literary texts |
| Psychological comfort | ✅ Yes | Provides a narrative frame under anxiety (also a risk) |
| Social conversation | ✅ Yes | Functions as social currency |

**The critical distinction:**

> Using it as a **mirror** → it asks "how do you see your own life?"
> Using it as a **manual** → it tells you "this is the only way you can live."

The first is harmless. The second is dangerous.

## 5. How to judge any reading yourself

A practical checklist. It works regardless of who is doing the reading.

### Check 1: Was anything stated in advance?

| Situation | Credibility |
|---|---|
| Specific predictions written down beforehand, then checked | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Discussable |
| Only post-hoc explanation | ⭐ No evidential value |
| Refuses to write specific predictions | ⭐ Be cautious |

### Check 2: Do they admit uncertainty?

A good practitioner will say:
- "I'm not sure about this one"
- "Different editions read differently here"
- "This is a traditional claim with no documentary basis"

If a practitioner **answers everything with total confidence** — be more careful, not less.

### Check 3: Do they manufacture fear?

**This is the most important red line.**

| Script | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "You face great disaster this year — it must be neutralised" | 🚩 High risk |
| "Without this ritual you will harm your family" | 🚩 High risk |
| "Only I can resolve your fate" | 🚩🚩 Typical fraud |
| "I'm not certain about this; judge for yourself" | ✅ Normal |

**Anything that uses fear to drive payment is not divination — it is fraud.**

### Check 4: Is the fee tied to "resolution"?

| Model | Verdict |
|---|---|
| One-time consultation fee for analysis | ✅ Normal business |
| Free reading → "you're in danger" → expensive ritual | 🚩 Fraud pattern |
| Tiered pricing by "difficulty of resolution" | 🚩🚩 Fraud |
| Guarantees "do this and your luck changes" | 🚩🚩🚩 Report it |

### Check 5: Can they explain how it is derived?

They need not publish the full algorithm, but should be able to explain:
- The basic logic of number derivation
- What information *kaoke* uses
- Which edition the verses come from

If they say "the heavenly secrets cannot be revealed" —
**what cannot be revealed is usually not a heavenly secret, it is a trade secret.**
