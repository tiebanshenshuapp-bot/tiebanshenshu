# Kaoke (考刻) and Dingfen (定分)

> Tieban Shenshu has a "derive a number, look up a verse" architecture,
> but **what truly distinguishes it from other systems is kaoke.**
> This page explains what it is, why it exists, and where the limits are.

---

## 1. Two terms first

### Ke (刻)

Traditional Chinese timekeeping divides the day into twelve *shichen* (時辰),
each two hours long.

Tieban Shenshu subdivides further:

```
1 shichen (2 hours)
        ↓ divided into 8
1 ke = 15 minutes
        ↓ subdivided into 15
1 fen = 1 minute
```

So one shichen = eight ke = 120 minutes.

> **Evidence level: classical text.** The subdivision of the shichen into *ke* is common
> in traditional calendrical and divination literature. The specific "eight ke per shichen,
> 15 minutes per ke" scheme is widely attested but not universally identical across sources.

### Dingfen (定分)

Subdividing within an established *ke* to reach a *fen*, further narrowing the birth moment.

**Kaoke + Dingfen = 考刻定分**, the calibration procedure.

---

## 2. Why does calibration exist?

The core problem is a **physical fact**:

> People born within the same shichen (a two-hour window) produce **identical four pillars**
> under the traditional charting method.

In pre-modern times many infants could be born within one shichen. Same chart — different lives.

The traditional explanation:

1. Two hours is too coarse; finer resolution is needed to differentiate
2. After subdividing, different ke/fen map to different verses
3. But recorded birth times were often imprecise (no accurate clocks)

**Hence kaoke**: instead of trusting the clock, ask about life facts.

---

## 3. How it is traditionally done

### The basic idea

```
Derive numbers from the known birth time → get a candidate range of ke/fen
              ↓
Ask several "life facts" (mainly family information)
              ↓
Within the candidate range, find the ke/fen consistent with those facts
              ↓
Fix the "true ke" → look up verses
```

### Traditional anchor categories

| Category | Typical question | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | What zodiac is your father? Mother? | Hard anchor (verifiable) |
| Siblings | How many siblings? What rank? | Hard anchor |
| Spouse | What year/zodiac is your spouse? | Hard anchor |
| Children | How many children? Which sexes? | Hard anchor |
| Temperament | What is your personality like? | Soft anchor (subjective) |
| Career | What industry do you work in? | Soft anchor |
| Health | Any chronic conditions? | Soft anchor |

**Hard anchors are traditionally considered more reliable**, since zodiac signs and headcounts
can be precisely verified, while personality descriptions are elastic.

> **Evidence level: traditional account.** The above is a **framing description** shared across
> schools. But *which* anchors are used, *how* they map, and *how* candidates are ranked
> differ between schools. **There is no single authoritative standard.**

## 4. The controversy (stated plainly)

Kaoke is the most distinctive feature of the system — and **the most criticised**.

### The critics' argument

> "You ask me what zodiac my father is, then tell me a verse saying my father is that zodiac.
> Is that prediction?"

This criticism **is methodologically valid**:

```
If the calibration is "tuned using known facts"
   → then verses matching those facts is inevitable, not prophetic
```

In statistical language: this is **overfitting to increase fit**.

### The defenders' response

The traditional response:

1. The calibration is not "arbitrary tuning" — it is narrowing within a **bounded candidate set**
2. After calibration, verses are output **as a whole set**, not individually tailored
3. The real test: **do the verses NOT used for calibration also hold?**

**This response is also logically coherent** — if calibration used only "parents' zodiacs,"
yet the resulting verses also accurately describe the marital situation (never asked about),
there would be genuine information content.

### Our position

> We do not take sides, but we insist on one distinction:
> **when judging accuracy, "before calibration" and "after calibration" mean very different things.**

See [Is it accurate?](accuracy.md).

## 5. Why schools disagree

If you check different sources, you will find:

| Question | Different answers |
|---|---|
| How many ke per shichen | Usually eight, but other schemes exist |
| Minutes per ke | Usually 15 |
| Which anchors to use | Differs by school |
| How ke maps to verse IDs | **Privately transmitted; accounts differ** |
| Whether true solar time is required | Some require it, some do not |

### Why

1. **Transmission mode**: long transmitted **master-to-disciple**, with no standard curriculum
2. **Textual gaps**: primary documents are scarce; manuscript copies diverge
3. **Later additions**: different eras added different material
4. **Commercial secrecy**: some practitioners deliberately withhold detail

**Conclusion**: a single agreed "standard Tieban Shenshu algorithm" **does not exist**.

> Any claim of "the only orthodox version" requires evidence. We have not seen such evidence.

## 8. One-line summary

> **Kaoke** exists to solve the physical problem that people born within the same two-hour
> shichen share an identical chart.
> The traditional method works backwards from known facts to pin the true *ke*.
> This is **methodologically contested**, and the contest itself is worth studying.
> The specific correspondence tables are **not public**, because schools differ and they are
> commercially implemented.
